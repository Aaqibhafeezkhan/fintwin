A stress test has two parts: deciding which companies are fragile, and translating a shock into cash. We build FinTwin, an open-source system-dynamics digital twin calibrated for each company from point-in-time SEC XBRL filings, and evaluate the two parts separately against 13 benchmarks. Frozen before COVID-19, the twin's stress score ranks the 2020-21 cash burn of 333 S&P 500 non-financial companies better than the Altman, Ohlson and Zmijewski scores ($\rho$ = 0.40 against 0.31 for Altman's Z$''$), and it separates 27 companies that filed for Chapter 11 in 2020 from S&P survivors of similar size with an AUC of 0.95 (Z$''$: 0.88). But a company's trailing free-cash-flow margin ranks cash burn far better ($\rho$ = 0.68); the twin's own scoring formula, applied to the opening balance sheet without simulation, matches it on bankruptcies; and when burn is measured against each company's pre-shock run-rate, neither the twin nor the distress scores rank the 2020 shock. The twin's value lies in its mechanics instead. Fed the revenue that actually followed, it identifies where a margin pass-through rule misjudges cash flow, with an incremental correlation of 0.30 (95% CI 0.25-0.36) across 2,661 firm-years from 2014 to 2023, positive in every year; in 2020 it tracked the operating cash flow of companies whose revenue fell by more than 10% with $\rho$ = 0.72 against 0.27 for the rule, although its point predictions are noisier than the rule's. Its revenue forecasts beat a seasonal random walk and exponential smoothing. Filing-based simulation is a tool for conditional analysis, not for predicting who will be hit. Pipeline, data and code are public.
