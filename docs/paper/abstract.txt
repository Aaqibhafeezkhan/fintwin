Financial digital twins simulate how a company's cash, debt and margins respond to a shock. We ask whether that simulation adds information that simple persistence rules do not. FinTwin is a system-dynamics model calibrated for each company from point-in-time SEC XBRL filings; we test it on a survivorship-free universe of 5,220 non-financial filers (20,431 firm-years, 2014-2024) with a census of 377 bankruptcy petitions. The main test feeds each twin the revenue that actually followed and predicts eight quarters of operating cash flow. On its own the twin is the least accurate of eight models: its median error is 11.3 percentage points of revenue, against 6.9 for a 16-quarter cash-flow margin. A statistic from the first version of this paper, the correlation of the twin's departures from a four-quarter margin with the realized ones (0.33), is reproduced by the 16-quarter margin alone (0.40): it measured mean reversion, not transmission. The twin's departures do carry information that none of six simple rules has: their correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is 0.22 (95% CI 0.20-0.24), and they survive an encompassing regression (t = 9.5). Out of sample they are worth little: adding 10-15% of them to the best rule lowers its error by 3.5%, but adding a simple cash-revenue regression the same way does almost as well; only for free cash flow does the twin improve on every simple rule in such a combination, by 0.21 percentage points. In ranking firms, simulation adds nothing: the twin's score predicts bankruptcy within two years with an AUC of 0.86, its scoring formula applied to the opening balance sheet reaches 0.87, and among already-weak firms the two are level. Filing-based simulation mostly encodes persistence; its marginal value is small and confined to translating a known shock into free cash flow.
