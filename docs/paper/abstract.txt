Financial digital twins simulate how a company's cash, debt and margins respond to a shock. We ask whether the simulation adds information that simple persistence rules do not. FinTwin is a system-dynamics model calibrated for each company from point-in-time SEC XBRL filings. We test it on a survivorship-free universe of 5,220 non-financial filers (21,545 firm-years, 2014-2024) with a census of bankruptcy filings. The primary test gives each twin the revenue that actually followed and predicts eight quarters of operating cash flow. In levels the twin is less accurate than every simple rule: its median error is 11.3 percentage points of revenue, against 6.9 for a 16-quarter cash-flow margin, because its cash flow responds to revenue 2.3 times as strongly as firms' does. A statistic from the first version of this paper (0.32) is exceeded by the 16-quarter margin alone (0.39): it measured mean reversion, not transmission. The twin's departures from the rules do carry information that none of six rules has: their correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is 0.22 (95% CI 0.19-0.24), and they keep a significant coefficient in encompassing regressions (t = 9.2 with firm and year clustering). Out of sample this information is worth little. Adding 0.10-0.15 of it to the 16-quarter margin lowers the error by 3.6%, but a cash-revenue regression added the same way does almost as well; for free cash flow, the twin's advantage over the best such combination is not significant after a correction for multiple comparisons. In ranking firms the simulation adds nothing: the twin's score predicts bankruptcy within two years with an AUC of 0.86, and its own scoring formula, applied to the opening balance sheet without simulation, reaches 0.87. Given each industry's realized revenue shock, the twin's fragility is associated with abnormal cash burn in the 2015-16, 2020 and 2022 downturns, a decomposition rather than a forecast. Filing-based simulation mostly encodes persistence; the information it adds is real in sample but too small to improve forecasts reliably.
