\begin{abstract}
\noindent Financial digital twins simulate how a company's cash, debt and margins respond to a shock. We ask whether that simulation adds information that simple persistence rules do not. \twin{} is a system-dynamics model calibrated for each company from point-in-time SEC XBRL filings; we test it on a survivorship-free universe of \UniverseN{} non-financial filers (\PanFirmYears{} firm-years, 2014--2024) with a census of \BkPanEv{} bankruptcy petitions. The main test feeds each twin the revenue that actually followed and predicts eight quarters of operating cash flow. On its own the twin is the least accurate of eight models: its median error is \TrMaeTone{} percentage points of revenue, against \TrMaeNthree{} for a 16-quarter cash-flow margin. A statistic from the first version of this paper, the correlation of the twin's departures from a four-quarter margin with the realized ones (\TrIncrTwin), is reproduced by the 16-quarter margin alone (\TrIncrSixteen): it measured mean reversion, not transmission. The twin's departures do carry information that none of six simple rules has: their correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is \TrTwinVsSixteen{} (95\% CI \TrTwinVsSixteenLo--\TrTwinVsSixteenHi), they survive an encompassing regression ($t$ = \TrEncTwinT), and adding \TrLambdaLo--\TrLambdaHi{} of them to the best rule, with the weight chosen on earlier years, lowers its out-of-sample error by \TrHybGainPct. In ranking firms, simulation adds nothing: the twin's score predicts bankruptcy within two years with an AUC of \BkPanTwin, its scoring formula applied to the opening balance sheet reaches \BkPanFormula, and among already-weak firms the two are level. Filing-based simulation mostly encodes persistence; its marginal value is small, real and confined to translating a known shock into cash.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} digital twin; system dynamics; corporate liquidity; cash-flow prediction; bankruptcy prediction; stress testing; XBRL; forecast encompassing.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G32, G33, G17, C53, C63, M41.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A corporate stress test asks a conditional question: \emph{if} revenue falls by a third for a year and debt markets close, does the company run out of cash, and when? Credit analysts usually answer it with ratios --- leverage, coverage, liquidity --- or with accounting distress scores \citep{beaver1966,altman1968,ohlson1980,zmijewski1984}. A simulation model promises more: it represents the mechanisms through which a shock becomes a cash shortfall (fixed costs, working capital, maturing debt, credit lines, the loss of market access) and runs them forward. System dynamics provides the language for such models \citep{forrester1961,sterman2000}, and engineering calls a simulation that is kept synchronized with its physical counterpart a digital twin \citep{grieves2017,tao2019,kritzinger2018}.

The question this paper asks is whether the simulation adds information. A model that runs a stock-and-flow structure over thousands of Monte Carlo paths is expensive to build and to explain; if a trailing cash-flow margin, a regression of cash on revenue or an accounting score computed from the same filings predicts as well, the structure is ornament. The first version of this paper reported that it did add information: fed the revenue that actually followed, the twin's departures from a four-quarter margin rule correlated with the realized departures at 0.30, positive in every year. Referees pointed out that the statistic rewards any predictor that is smoother than the rule, because margins mean-revert \citep{fama2000} and the twin's costs are estimated over 16 quarters. They were right: a 16-quarter margin, with no simulation at all, scores \TrIncrSixteen{} on the same statistic.

This version therefore tests the twin against the rivals that matter. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets out how the twin relates to distress scores, structural credit models, financial-planning models and top-down stress tests, and states three hypotheses. The data are every non-financial SEC filer with at least \$50 million of revenue, including the ones that later disappeared, and every bankruptcy petition they filed (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The primary test (Section~\ref{sec:design}) is a single one: given the realized revenue path, predict cumulative operating cash flow over eight quarters, and compare the twin with six simple rules that see the same revenue --- margins over 4, 8 and 16 quarters, an industry-shrunk margin, and per-firm regressions of cash flow on revenue with and without working-capital timing. The decision rule for accepting the transmission claim was fixed and committed to the repository before the test was run.

The results are mixed in a way that is informative. In levels the twin is the worst of the eight models (Section~\ref{sec:transmission}): its fixed-cost structure makes it overreact to revenue changes. Its departures from the rules nevertheless contain information that none of the rules has, and a forecast that adds a small fraction of them to the 16-quarter margin is more accurate out of sample than the margin alone and than the margin combined with the best regression rule. The improvement is about \TrHybGainPct{} of the error, or half a percentage point of annual revenue over two years. In ranking firms by distress (Section~\ref{sec:ranking}), the twin's score is as good as the best accounting score and no better than its own scoring formula evaluated without simulation, including in the hard task of telling which already-weak firms will file. The feedback loops do not change rankings but are the only channel through which the model produces cash depletion and new borrowing, and they improve the prediction of those liquidity events slightly (Section~\ref{sec:decisions}). The 2020 shock is one episode among eleven; it shows that, once each firm is given its industry's realized revenue shock, fragility predicts the abnormal cash burn that a uniform scenario could not (Section~\ref{sec:covid}). Probabilistic forecasts are well calibrated on average after conformal rescaling but not in the stress year (Section~\ref{sec:forecasts}), and the main results are insensitive to the model's assumed parameters except for the prior on fixed costs (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

The paper makes three contributions. It shows how easily a simulation model's apparent skill can come from persistence, and provides a ladder of simple rules, an encompassing test \citep{chong1986,fair1990} and an out-of-sample combination \citep{bates1969} that separate the two. It provides an open, survivorship-free, point-in-time panel of all non-financial XBRL filers with a census of bankruptcy petitions, on which fundamental models can be tested without hindsight. And it gives a measured answer to what a filing-based financial twin is for: scenario analysis of a specified shock, with its output shrunk toward persistence, not early warning.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The primary test: transmission}
For every firm and every cutoff (15 February 2014--2024), the twin is calibrated on the filings available at the cutoff. The revenue the company actually reported over the eight following quarters then replaces the twin's simulated revenue, 500 paths are run, and the median path of quarterly operating cash flow (and free cash flow) is taken. The outcome is cumulative operating cash flow over the eight quarters as a percentage of revenue in the four quarters before the cutoff. This isolates transmission --- how a given revenue path becomes cash --- from the scenario, which no filing can predict. Every rival sees the same realized revenue, quarter by quarter:
\begin{enumerate}[label=N\arabic*]
\item the trailing four-quarter cash-flow margin times revenue (the rule used in the first version);
\item the same over eight quarters, and
\item over sixteen quarters, the same window as the twin's cost regression;
\item the four-quarter margin shrunk toward the leave-one-out median margin of the firm's industry in the same year, with the weight chosen each year to minimize errors on earlier years only;
\item a per-firm regression of quarterly cash flow on revenue over 16 quarters (a fixed and a variable part: operating leverage without simulation);
\item the same regression with the change in revenue added (working-capital timing).
\end{enumerate}
\noindent T0 is the twin with its four feedback loops switched off and T1 the full twin. N5 and N6 are the ``simpler dynamic financial models'' a referee asked about, and N6 also answers the point that the twin, unlike a margin rule, sees the timing of revenue: so does N6.

We report four statistics. \emph{Errors in levels}: median and mean absolute error (outcomes winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles for the mean). \emph{Incremental correlation}: the Spearman correlation between a model's departure from a base rule and the realized departure from it, against every base, not only the weakest. \emph{Encompassing}: an OLS regression of the realized departure from N1 on the departures of the twin, N3, N4 and N6, with year effects and firm-clustered errors; a significant twin coefficient means its forecast contains information the others do not \citep{chong1986,fair1990}. \emph{Out-of-sample combination}: for test year $Y$, the prediction $\text{N}k+\lambda(\text{T1}-\text{N}k)$ with $\lambda$ chosen on years before $Y$ \citep{bates1969}, compared with N$k$ alone and with the same combination built from a simple rule.

\paragraph{Decision rule.} H2 is accepted only if the twin's incremental correlation against each of N3, N4 and N6 has a 95\% confidence interval above zero and its encompassing coefficient is significant at 5\%. The rule was committed to the repository before the full-sample run (commit \texttt{460cdda}). An earlier draft of the rule also required the best simple rule to reach less than 80\% of the twin's statistic against N1; it was replaced before the run because a synthetic test showed it rejects a twin with genuine information whenever the four-quarter rule is very noisy.

\subsection{Secondary tests}
\emph{Liquidity decisions.} The same runs give the share of paths on which cash falls below half its opening level, net new borrowing exceeds 5\% of revenue, dividends are cut by half or capital expenditure by a quarter. They are compared with the realized events and with a static runway calculation that uses the same inputs: the trailing free-cash-flow margin on the realized revenue, minus the current payout run-rate and the maturities the company does not usually refinance. Each loop is switched off in turn.

\emph{Ranking.} Without the realized revenue, the twin's baseline health score at each cutoff is compared with the accounting benchmarks for five events over the next eight quarters: a bankruptcy petition, severe raw and abnormal cash burn (cumulative free cash flow below $-10\%$ of revenue, raw or after subtracting the firm's own trailing run-rate), a fall in interest coverage below 1.5$\times$, and a dividend cut. The \emph{health formula} --- the twin's scoring rule applied to the opening balance sheet --- isolates what the simulation adds to the score's ingredients. The hard version of the bankruptcy test restricts the sample to firms that are already weak: the bottom quintile of Altman's Z$''$ in the cutoff year or a negative free-cash-flow margin.

\emph{Benchmarks.} Altman's Z$''$ \citep{altman2006}, Ohlson's O-score, Zmijewski's score, interest coverage, net debt to EBITDA, cash to revenue, the current ratio, revenue volatility, the estimated fixed-cost share, size, a static stress test of the demand-freeze scenario, and the two persistence benchmarks: the trailing free-cash-flow margin and capital expenditure over revenue. All are computed from the same vintage as the twin and oriented so that higher means riskier.

\subsection{Inference}
Bootstrap confidence intervals resample firms (500 resamples in the panel, 2{,}000 in cross-sections); comparisons between predictors are paired. Out-of-sample logits are ridge-penalized and, for test year $Y$, trained on cutoffs up to $Y-3$, whose eight-quarter outcomes were known by $Y$; percentile ranks are computed within each cutoff year. We run many comparisons; individual $p$-values near 0.05 should be read with that in mind, and the decision rule above is the only test we treat as confirmatory.

\section{Results: transmission}\label{sec:transmission}

\subsection{Levels}
Table~\ref{tab:transmission} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} report the \TrN{} firm-years (\TrFirms{} firms) with eight quarters of realized data. Accuracy in levels falls almost monotonically with model complexity. The 16-quarter margin is the most accurate model, with a median absolute error of \TrMaeNthree{} percentage points of revenue; the four-quarter margin reaches \TrMaeNone, the regressions \TrMaeNfive{} and \TrMaeNsix, and the twin \TrMaeTone. Removing the feedback loops changes little (\TrMaeTzero). The twin's error comes mostly from overreaction: its cost structure, estimated with a prior that 35\% of costs are fixed, turns revenue changes into larger cash changes than firms experience (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Information beyond the rules}
The first version's statistic, the incremental correlation against the four-quarter margin, is \TrIncrTwin{} for the twin (95\% CI \TrIncrTwinLo--\TrIncrTwinHi), close to the 0.30 reported then. Every simple rule scores almost as well, and the 16-quarter margin scores higher (\TrIncrSixteen, CI \TrIncrSixteenLo--\TrIncrSixteenHi): against a noisy base, any less noisy estimate of the persistent margin looks informative. This is the null the referees proposed, and it accounts for all of the original result.

Against the stronger bases the twin still carries information. Its incremental correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is \TrTwinVsSixteen{} (CI \TrTwinVsSixteenLo--\TrTwinVsSixteenHi), with the residual of the shrunk margin \TrTwinVsShrunk, and with the residual of the regression with timing \TrTwinVsReg. These numbers alone are not decisive --- the four-quarter margin itself has an incremental correlation of \TrFourVsSixteen{} against the 16-quarter margin, because two noisy estimates of the same margin are partly independent --- which is why the encompassing regression is the confirmatory test. With the twin, the 16-quarter margin, the shrunk margin and the timing regression in the same regression (Table~\ref{tab:encompassing}), each keeps a significant coefficient; the twin's $t$-statistic is \TrEncTwinT{} for operating and \TrEncFcfTwinT{} for free cash flow. By the pre-specified rule, H2 \TrVerdict.

\subsection{How much it is worth}
Information that is significant in a regression of 16{,}000 observations need not be worth much. Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} measures it out of sample. Combining the 16-quarter margin with a fraction $\lambda$ of the twin's departure, with $\lambda$ chosen each year on earlier years only, lowers the mean absolute error from \TrHybSixteen{} to \TrHybSixteenTwin{} percentage points of revenue, a gain of \TrHybGain{} (CI \TrHybGainLo--\TrHybGainHi), or \TrHybGainPct{} of the error (\TrFcfHybGainPct{} for free cash flow). The same combination built from the timing regression instead of the twin reaches \TrHybSixteenReg; the twin's version is better by \TrHybVsNull{} ($p\TrHybVsNullP$). The estimated weight is small and stable, between \TrLambdaLo{} and \TrLambdaHi{} in every year: the direction of the twin's departures is informative, their size is not. For a company with \$10 billion of revenue, the gain is about \$45 million in the predicted cash flow of the next two years --- relevant for a liquidity plan built on a stated revenue scenario, negligible for a credit decision.

\paragraph{Examples.} The size of the departures explains the small weight. In 2020, Amazon's revenue over the next eight quarters averaged 53\% above its trailing level; the 16-quarter margin implied cumulative operating cash flow of 39\% of trailing revenue, the twin 63\%, and the company reported 40\%. The twin had the direction right for General Electric in 2016 (rule 26\%, twin 15\%, realized 7\%) and wrong for Ford and General Motors in 2020, where a revenue fall of 15--19\% made the twin predict operating cash flow near zero while both companies' reported operating cash flow rose; their consolidated cash flows include captive finance operations that a revenue-driven model does not represent. Across firm-years with large departures (more than 8 points) by firms with more than \$2 billion of revenue, the twin's departure has the sign of the realized one 58\% of the time.

\subsection{Where it helps}
A referee asked whether the twin is more useful for highly leveraged firms, firms with high fixed costs or large maturities, or in severe revenue contractions. Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity} gives little support. Its incremental correlation against the timing regression does not rise with leverage (\HetLevLowTwinReg{} in the lowest tercile, \HetLevHighTwinReg{} in the highest) and differs little across terciles of fixed costs and maturities. Among the \HetDownN{} firm-years with revenue more than 10\% below its trailing level, the twin's incremental correlation against the four-quarter rule (\HetDownTwin) is lower than the 16-quarter margin's (\HetDownSixteen). Year by year (Figure~\ref{fig:structural}), the twin's statistic against the four-quarter rule is below the 16-quarter margin's in most years, and its statistic against the timing regression is stable around 0.4.

\section{Results: liquidity decisions and feedback loops}\label{sec:decisions}
Ranking correlations are not what a risk manager uses. Table~\ref{tab:decisions} asks whether, given the revenue that followed, the twin predicts the decisions and events that a liquidity plan is about. Its event probabilities are weak predictors in absolute terms (AUC 0.56--0.68), comparable with the static runway rule and the opening-balance-sheet score, and they add a small amount out of sample to a logit of those rivals: \DecCashDelta{} in AUC for cash falling below half, \DecDebtDelta{} for new borrowing, \DecDivDelta{} for dividend cuts and \DecCapexDelta{} for capital-expenditure cuts, all with confidence intervals above zero.

Table~\ref{tab:loops} evaluates each loop on these outcomes, as a referee suggested. The result is mechanical but informative about the model's structure. Without the market-access cliff (R2), the twin's cash practically never falls below half its opening level, because the treasury always refinances up to its target; the cash-depletion AUC falls from \DecCashTwin{} to \DecCashNoRtwo. Without cash conservation (B1), dividends are never cut and capital expenditure is cut less often (AUC \DecCapexNoBone{} against \DecCapexTwin). The spread spiral (R1) and delayed restructuring (B2) change no outcome. Two of the four loops are therefore necessary for the model to produce the liquidity events at all; the other two are inert over an eight-quarter horizon. None of them changes the ranking of firms (Section~\ref{sec:ranking}).

\section{Results: ranking firms}\label{sec:ranking}
Table~\ref{tab:bankruptcy} reports the bankruptcy test on the census. Across \BkPanN{} firm-years with \BkPanEv{} petitions within eight quarters, the twin's baseline score has an AUC of \BkPanTwin, against \BkPanFormula{} for its own formula on the opening balance sheet (difference \BkPanFormulaDiff, $p\BkPanFormulaP$), \BkPanOhlson{} for Ohlson's O-score and \BkPanAltman{} for Altman's Z$''$. Size, which separated the 2020 bankruptcies from S\&P members in the first version, now reaches only \BkPanSize. The hard task is the one a referee emphasized: \BkWeakShare{} of the petitions come from the \BkWeakN{} firm-years that were already weak. Among them the twin reaches \BkWeakTwin{} and the formula \BkWeakFormula{} ($p\BkWeakFormulaP$ for the difference); Altman's Z$''$ falls to \BkWeakAltman. The same pattern holds for the \BkTwentyEv{} petitions after 20 February 2020 (twin \BkTwentyTwin, formula \BkTwentyFormula). The twin's score is a good distress score; the simulation behind it adds nothing to the ranking.

\section{Forecasts and calibration}\label{sec:forecasts}
For S\&P~500 members (today's and those of 2020), the twin's four-quarter predictive distributions of revenue improve on a seasonal random walk by \FcRevRw{} and on exponential smoothing by \FcRevEts{} in scaled CRPS ($t$ = \FcRevRwT{} and \FcRevEtsT; Table~\ref{tab:fc}); for cash both benchmarks are better (\FcCashRw{} and \FcCashEts).

Raw 90\% intervals are too narrow: they cover \CalRevRaw{} of revenue outcomes and \CalCashRaw{} of cash outcomes. Online conformal rescaling, using only errors that had been reported by each forecast origin, restores coverage to \CalRevConf{} and \CalCashConf{} on average (Table~\ref{tab:cal}, Figure~\ref{fig:cal}). The referee's question was whether this holds where it matters. It holds across horizons and firm sizes, but not in the stress year: for quarters from 2020Q2 to 2021Q1, conformal revenue intervals cover only \CalRevStress. The recalibration learns the average error of quiet years and cannot anticipate a regime break. For cash, the raw intervals do not widen for smaller firms ($t$ = \CalCashWidthSizeT{} for size in a regression of interval width) although their errors are larger ($t$ = \CalCashErrSizeT): the uncertainty model is too homogeneous across firms.

\section{Sensitivity and hindsight}\label{sec:sensitivity}
Several parameters are judgement calls rather than estimates: the credit line assumed when none is tagged, the prior on the fixed-cost share and its weight, the shrinkage of trend growth and cyclicality, the coverage thresholds of the market-access cliff, the speed of restructuring, and the rating-spread table. Table~\ref{tab:sens} varies each in turn. The incremental correlation against the four-quarter rule ranges from \SensIncrLo{} to \SensIncrHi, and the bankruptcy AUC from \SensBkLo{} to \SensBkHi. The one assumption that matters is the fixed-cost prior: with a prior of 20\% instead of 35\% the twin's median error falls to \SensMaeLow{} points, with 50\% it rises to \SensMaeHigh. The data prefer less operating leverage than the model assumes, which is the overreaction seen in Section~\ref{sec:transmission}. We report the specified model and do not retune it on the test sample.

Hindsight enters in three places, each now addressed or measured. GDP enters as the vintage published on each cutoff. Rating spreads come from a 2026 table; rescaling them by the Baa spread observed at each cutoff (last row of Table~\ref{tab:sens}) changes nothing. The model's structure and priors were designed in 2026 with knowledge of the 2014--2024 data, which no backtest can undo; the decision rule for H2 was fixed before the full-sample test, and the weekly rebuild of the public twin now accumulates genuinely prospective predictions.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Statistical, predictive and decision value.} The three are different, and the evidence separates them. The twin's transmission mechanics are statistically informative beyond persistence (a $t$-statistic of 10 in the encompassing regression). Their predictive value is small: a few percent of the error of the best simple rule, and only when shrunk to about a sixth of their size. Their decision value lies where a simple rule has no answer at all: the timing of a breach when maturities and credit lines interact, the amount of new borrowing a stated scenario requires, whether a dividend survives. On those questions the twin is a structured calculator whose inputs are estimated and audited from filings, and its advantage over a static runway calculation is small but positive.

\paragraph{What the twin is for.} A filing-based twin is not an early-warning system: its ranking of firms is that of its own scoring formula. It is a tool for conditional analysis of a specified shock, and its output should be shrunk toward persistence --- in practice, a cash forecast should start from the long-window margin and adjust a fraction of the way toward the simulation.

\paragraph{External validity.} The universe is US non-financial SEC filers with at least \$50 million of revenue. The pipeline depends on standardized, tagged, point-in-time disclosure, which exists in a comparable form only under SEC XBRL and, since 2020--21, the European Single Electronic Format for annual reports of listed companies (without quarterly data). Smaller and private firms report less often and less consistently, which affects calibration more than structure: the stock-and-flow core and the accounting identity carry over, but parameters estimated from 16 quarters would have to come from fewer annual observations, with more weight on priors. Banks and insurers need a different balance-sheet model. Market-based inputs, which structural credit models use, are a natural extension for listed firms.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The transmission test uses realized revenue, so it cannot separate errors in the model's mechanics from noise in reported cash flow. Bankruptcy is observed only through 8-K filings; firms that stop filing before petitioning, or that are acquired in distress, are not counted. Outcomes for firms that disappear are missing, so the transmission and burn tests condition on survival for eight quarters. Some XBRL items are tagged idiosyncratically.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We built a transparent, automatically calibrated simulation model of the firm and asked what it knows that simple rules do not. Most of what it appeared to know was persistence, and the test that first suggested otherwise measured mean reversion. What remains is small and specific: given a revenue path, the direction of the simulation's departures from a long-window cash-flow margin is informative, and adding a sixth of them to the margin improves cash-flow predictions out of sample. The simulation does not help to rank firms by distress. These answers come from a design that any simulation model of the firm can be held to --- a ladder of simple rivals, an encompassing test and an out-of-sample combination, on a survivorship-free panel with a census of defaults --- and the data, code and tests are public so that it can be.

\section{Implementation}
