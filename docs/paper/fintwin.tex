\begin{abstract}
\noindent Financial digital twins simulate how a company's cash, debt and margins respond to a shock. We ask whether that simulation adds information that simple persistence rules do not. \twin{} is a system-dynamics model calibrated for each company from point-in-time SEC XBRL filings. We test it on a survivorship-free universe of \UniverseN{} non-financial filers (\PanFirmYears{} firm-years, 2014--2024) with a census of \BkPanEv{} bankruptcy petitions. The main test feeds each twin the revenue that actually followed and predicts eight quarters of operating cash flow. On its own the twin is the least accurate of eight models: its median error is \TrMaeTone{} percentage points of revenue, against \TrMaeNthree{} for a 16-quarter cash-flow margin. A statistic from the first version of this paper, the correlation of the twin's departures from a four-quarter margin with the realized ones (\TrIncrTwin), is reproduced by the 16-quarter margin alone (\TrIncrSixteen): it measured mean reversion, not transmission. The twin's departures do carry information that none of six simple rules has: their correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is \TrTwinVsSixteen{} (95\% CI \TrTwinVsSixteenLo--\TrTwinVsSixteenHi), and they survive an encompassing regression ($t$ = \TrEncLevTwinT). Out of sample they are worth little: adding \TrLambdaLo--\TrLambdaHi{} of them to the best rule lowers its error by \TrHybGainPct, but adding a simple cash-revenue regression the same way does almost as well. Only for free cash flow does the twin improve on every simple rule in such a combination, by \TrFcfVsBestNull{} percentage points. The twin overreacts: its cash flow responds to revenue \ElastCfoRatio{} times as strongly as firms' does, because its prior on fixed costs is too high; a prior selected walk-forward lowers its median error from \RevMaeBase{} to \RevMaeNew{} points, still above the margin's. In ranking firms, simulation adds nothing. The twin's score predicts bankruptcy within two years with an AUC of \BkPanTwin; its scoring formula applied to the opening balance sheet reaches \BkPanFormula; among already-weak firms the two are level. Given each firm's industry shock, however, the twin's fragility predicts which firms burned more cash than usual in the 2015--16, 2020 and 2022 downturns. Filing-based simulation mostly encodes persistence; its marginal value is small and confined to translating a known shock into free cash flow.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} digital twin; system dynamics; corporate liquidity; cash-flow prediction; bankruptcy prediction; stress testing; XBRL; forecast encompassing.

\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G32, G33, G17, C53, C63, M41.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A corporate stress test asks a conditional question: \emph{if} revenue falls by a third for a year and debt markets close, does the company run out of cash, and when? Credit analysts usually answer it with ratios (leverage, coverage, liquidity) or with accounting distress scores \citep{beaver1966,altman1968,ohlson1980,zmijewski1984}. A simulation model promises more: it represents the mechanisms through which a shock becomes a cash shortfall (fixed costs, working capital, maturing debt, credit lines, the loss of market access) and runs them forward. System dynamics provides the language for such models \citep{forrester1961,sterman2000}, and engineering calls a simulation that is kept synchronized with its physical counterpart a digital twin \citep{grieves2017,tao2019,kritzinger2018}.

This paper asks whether the simulation adds information. A model that runs a stock-and-flow structure over thousands of Monte Carlo paths is expensive to build and to explain. If a trailing cash-flow margin, a regression of cash on revenue or an accounting score computed from the same filings predicts as well, the structure is ornament. The first version of this paper reported that it did add information: fed the revenue that actually followed, the twin's departures from a four-quarter margin rule correlated with the realized departures at 0.30, and the correlation was positive in every year. Readers of that version pointed out that the statistic rewards any predictor that is smoother than the rule, because profitability mean-reverts \citep{fama2000} and the twin's costs are estimated over 16 quarters. They were right: a 16-quarter margin, with no simulation at all, scores \TrIncrSixteen{} on the same statistic.

This version therefore tests the twin against stronger rivals. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets out how the twin relates to distress scores, structural credit models, financial-planning models and top-down stress tests, and states three hypotheses. The data cover every non-financial SEC filer with at least \$50 million of revenue, including those that later disappeared, and every bankruptcy petition these firms filed (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The primary test (Section~\ref{sec:design}) is a single one: given the realized revenue path, predict cumulative operating cash flow over eight quarters. The twin is compared with six simple rules that see the same revenue: margins over 4, 8 and 16 quarters, an industry-shrunk margin, and per-firm regressions of cash flow on revenue with and without working-capital timing. We fixed the decision rule for accepting the transmission claim, and committed it to the repository, before running the test.

The results are mixed. In levels the twin is the worst of the eight models (Section~\ref{sec:transmission}): its fixed-cost structure makes it overreact to revenue changes. Its departures from the rules nevertheless contain information, in a regression sense, that none of the rules has. Out of sample that information is worth little. A forecast that adds a small fraction of the departures to the 16-quarter margin is about \TrHybGainPct{} more accurate than the margin alone, but most of that gain is the familiar benefit of combining two forecasts: a simple regression rule added in the same way achieves almost as much. Only for free cash flow (FCF) does the twin's contribution exceed that of every simple rule. In ranking firms by distress (Section~\ref{sec:ranking}), the twin's score is as good as the best accounting score and no better than its own scoring formula evaluated without simulation. This also holds for the hard task of telling which already-weak firms will file. The feedback loops do not change rankings. They are, however, the only channel through which the model produces cash depletion and new borrowing, and they slightly improve the prediction of those liquidity events (Section~\ref{sec:decisions}). The 2020 shock is now one cutoff among eleven. Once each firm is given its industry's realized revenue shock, fragility predicts the abnormal cash burn that a uniform scenario could not (Section~\ref{sec:covid}). After conformal rescaling, probabilistic forecasts are well calibrated on average but not in the stress year (Section~\ref{sec:forecasts}). The main results are insensitive to the model's assumed parameters except for the prior on fixed costs (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

The paper makes three contributions. First, it shows how easily a simulation model's apparent skill can come from persistence, and it provides a ladder of simple rules, an encompassing test \citep{chong1986,fair1990} and an out-of-sample combination \citep{bates1969} that separate the two. Second, it provides an open, survivorship-free, point-in-time panel of all non-financial XBRL filers with a census of bankruptcy petitions, on which fundamental models can be tested without hindsight. Third, it gives a measured answer to what a filing-based financial twin is for: scenario analysis of a specified shock, with its output shrunk toward persistence, rather than early warning.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The primary test: transmission}
For every firm and every cutoff (15 February 2014--2024), the twin is calibrated on the filings available at the cutoff. We then replace the twin's simulated revenue with the revenue the company actually reported over the following eight quarters, run 500 paths and take the median path of quarterly operating cash flow (and of FCF). The outcome is cumulative operating cash flow over the eight quarters as a percentage of revenue in the four quarters before the cutoff. This isolates transmission --- how a given revenue path becomes cash --- from the scenario, which no filing can predict. Every rival sees the same realized revenue, quarter by quarter:
\begin{enumerate}[label=N\arabic*]
\item the trailing four-quarter cash-flow margin times revenue (the rule used in the first version);
\item the same over eight quarters;
\item the same over 16 quarters, the window of the twin's cost regression;
\item the four-quarter margin shrunk toward the leave-one-out median margin of the firm's industry in the same year, with the weight chosen each year to minimize errors on earlier years only;
\item a per-firm regression of quarterly cash flow on revenue over 16 quarters (a fixed and a variable part: operating leverage without simulation);
\item the same regression with the change in revenue added (working-capital timing).
\end{enumerate}
\noindent T0 is the twin with its four feedback loops switched off; T1 is the full twin. N5 and N6 are simpler dynamic financial models: a fixed and a variable cost, and working-capital timing, without simulation. N6 also answers the point that the twin, unlike a margin rule, sees the timing of revenue: so does N6.

We report four statistics. \emph{Errors in levels}: median and mean absolute error (outcomes winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles for the mean). \emph{Incremental correlation}: the Spearman correlation between a model's departure from a base rule and the realized departure from it, computed against every base, not only the weakest. \emph{Encompassing}: an OLS regression of the realized departure from N1 on the departures of the twin, N3, N4 and N6, with year effects and firm-clustered errors; a significant twin coefficient means its forecast contains information the others do not \citep{chong1986,fair1990}. \emph{Out-of-sample combination}: for test year $Y$, the prediction $\text{N}k+\lambda(\text{T1}-\text{N}k)$ with $\lambda$ chosen on years before $Y$ \citep{bates1969}, compared with N$k$ alone and with the same combination built from a simple rule.

\paragraph{Decision rule.} H2 is accepted only if the twin's incremental correlation against each of N3, N4 and N6 has a 95\% confidence interval above zero and its encompassing coefficient is significant at 5\%. The rule was committed to the repository before the full-sample run (commit \texttt{460cdda}). An earlier draft of the rule also required the best simple rule to reach less than 80\% of the twin's statistic against N1. We replaced it before the run because a synthetic test showed that it rejects a twin with genuine information whenever the four-quarter margin is very noisy. That draft was never committed, so only the final rule can be verified in the repository history.

\paragraph{Second round.} After the first results we added a second set of tests and committed their decision rules before running them (commit \texttt{7a5d9b1}). They cover a revised specification, the timing and thresholds of liquidity events, other downturns, the mechanism behind the FCF result, group-conditional and adaptive calibration, two-way clustering and small firms. After that run we found and corrected one implementation error: the adaptive-conformal update was applied per forecast instead of per batch of yearly forecasts. The rule itself was not changed.

\paragraph{Specifications added after the run.} Three analyses were added after the first full-sample results and are exploratory: (i) the combinations built on the 16-quarter margin; (ii) the unconstrained encompassing regression in levels; and (iii) the combinations of the 16-quarter margin with each simple rule, which control for the gains that any combination of forecasts delivers \citep{bates1969,clemen1989,timmermann2006}. The pre-specified combination used the four-quarter margin; we chose the 16-quarter margin as a base because it turned out to be the most accurate rule.

\subsection{Secondary tests}
\emph{Liquidity decisions.} The same runs give the share of paths on which cash falls below half its opening level, net new borrowing exceeds 5\% of revenue, dividends are cut by half or capital expenditure by a quarter. They are compared with the realized events and with a static runway calculation that uses the same inputs: the trailing FCF margin applied to the realized revenue, minus the current payout run-rate and the maturities the company does not usually refinance. Each loop is switched off in turn.

\emph{Ranking.} Without the realized revenue, the twin's baseline health score at each cutoff is compared with the accounting benchmarks for five events over the next eight quarters: a bankruptcy petition, severe raw and abnormal cash burn (cumulative FCF below $-10\%$ of revenue, raw or after subtracting the firm's own trailing run-rate), a fall in interest coverage below 1.5$\times$, and a dividend cut. The \emph{health formula} --- the twin's scoring rule applied to the opening balance sheet --- isolates what the simulation adds to the score's ingredients. Its runway component is computed without simulation: cash and undrawn credit lines divided by one quarter of fixed costs, interest and the share of maturing debt that coverage would not allow to be refinanced. The hard version of the bankruptcy test restricts the sample to firms that are already weak: the bottom quintile of Altman's Z$''$ in the cutoff year or a negative FCF margin.

\emph{Benchmarks.} Altman's Z$''$ \citep{altman2006}, Ohlson's O-score, Zmijewski's score, interest coverage, net debt to EBITDA, cash to revenue, the current ratio, revenue volatility, the estimated fixed-cost share, size, a static stress test of the demand-freeze scenario, and the two persistence benchmarks: the trailing FCF margin and capital expenditure over revenue. All are computed from the same vintage as the twin and oriented so that higher means riskier.

\subsection{Inference}
Bootstrap confidence intervals resample firms (500 resamples in the panel, 2{,}000 in cross-sections); comparisons between predictors are paired. Out-of-sample logits are ridge-penalized and, for test year $Y$, trained on cutoffs up to $Y-3$, whose eight-quarter outcomes were known by $Y$; percentile ranks are computed within each cutoff year. Common shocks within a year enter the regressions as year effects. With eleven cutoffs, however, the intervals do not account for further cross-sectional dependence and may be somewhat narrow. A pigeonhole bootstrap that resamples firms and years independently gives the twin's incremental correlation against the 16-quarter margin a confidence interval of \TwoWayLo--\TwoWayHi. We run many comparisons. Only the tests governed by the decision rules above are confirmatory; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:holm} reports Holm-adjusted $p$-values for the secondary comparisons.

\section{Results: transmission}\label{sec:transmission}

\subsection{Levels}
Table~\ref{tab:transmission} and Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} report the \TrN{} firm-years (\TrFirms{} firms) with eight quarters of realized data. Accuracy in levels falls almost monotonically with model complexity. The 16-quarter margin is the most accurate model, with a median absolute error of \TrMaeNthree{} percentage points of revenue; the four-quarter margin reaches \TrMaeNone, the regressions \TrMaeNfive{} and \TrMaeNsix, and the twin \TrMaeTone. Removing the feedback loops changes little (\TrMaeTzero). The twin's error comes mostly from overreaction: its cost structure, estimated with a prior that 35\% of costs are fixed, turns revenue changes into larger cash changes than firms experience (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Information beyond the rules}
The first version's statistic, the incremental correlation against the four-quarter margin, is \TrIncrTwin{} for the twin (95\% CI \TrIncrTwinLo--\TrIncrTwinHi), close to the 0.30 reported then. Every simple rule scores almost as well, and the 16-quarter margin scores higher (\TrIncrSixteen, CI \TrIncrSixteenLo--\TrIncrSixteenHi): against a noisy base, any less noisy estimate of the persistent margin looks informative. The original statistic therefore cannot distinguish the twin from a smoother estimate of the margin.

Against the stronger bases the twin still carries information. Its incremental correlation with the residual of the 16-quarter margin is \TrTwinVsSixteen{} (CI \TrTwinVsSixteenLo--\TrTwinVsSixteenHi), with the residual of the shrunk margin \TrTwinVsShrunk, and with the residual of the regression with timing \TrTwinVsReg. These numbers alone are not decisive. The four-quarter margin itself has an incremental correlation of \TrFourVsSixteen{} against the 16-quarter margin, because two noisy estimates of the same margin are partly independent; this is why the encompassing regression is the confirmatory test. When the twin, the 16-quarter margin, the shrunk margin and the timing regression enter the same regression (Table~\ref{tab:encompassing}), each keeps a significant coefficient; the twin's $t$-statistic is \TrEncTwinT{} for operating cash flow and \TrEncFcfTwinT{} for FCF. By the pre-specified rule, H2 \TrVerdict. Because every term in that regression is measured relative to the four-quarter margin, the rule's noise enters the dependent variable and every regressor with the same sign, and the rule's own weight is restricted to one minus the sum of the others. The unconstrained form \citep{fair1990}, which regresses realized cash flow on all five forecasts in levels, gives the same answer: the twin's coefficient is \TrEncLevTwinCoef{} with $t$ = \TrEncLevTwinT{} (\TrEncLevFcfTwinT{} for FCF). In that regression the four-quarter margin receives a negative weight once the 16-quarter margin is included, the signature of mean reversion.

\subsection{How much it is worth}
Information that is significant in a regression of about \TrNRound{} observations need not be worth much. Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} measures it out of sample. Combining the 16-quarter margin with a fraction $\lambda$ of the twin's departure, with $\lambda$ chosen each year on earlier years only, lowers the mean absolute error from \TrHybSixteen{} to \TrHybSixteenTwin{} percentage points of revenue, a gain of \TrHybGain{} (CI \TrHybGainLo--\TrHybGainHi), or \TrHybGainPct{} of the error (\TrFcfHybGainPct{} for FCF). Combining forecasts usually helps even when the second forecast is worse, because their errors are imperfectly correlated \citep{bates1969,clemen1989,timmermann2006}. The relevant comparison is therefore with the 16-quarter margin combined in the same way with a simple rule. For operating cash flow, that comparison removes most of the gain. The margin combined with the cash-revenue regression reaches \TrHybSixteenRegFive{} (an improvement of \TrHybRegFiveGainPct), and the twin's combination is better by only \TrHybVsRegFive{} ($p\TrHybVsRegFiveP$); against the margin combined with the four-quarter margin, it is better by \TrHybVsFour{} ($p\TrHybVsFourP$). For FCF, the twin's combination is better than the best combination with a simple rule by \TrFcfVsBestNull{} percentage points (CI \TrFcfVsBestNullLo--\TrFcfVsBestNullHi) and better than each of them ($p\TrFcfVsWorstP$ or lower). We had conjectured that this advantage comes from capital expenditure (capex), which the twin cuts under stress and the cash rules do not. Combining capex and operating cash flow separately rejects that conjecture: capex accounts for only \FcfCapexShare{} of the gain, and most of the gain appears only when the two are combined. The estimated weight is small and stable, between \TrLambdaLo{} and \TrLambdaHi{} in every year: the direction of the twin's departures is informative, their size is not. For a company with \$10 billion of revenue, the gain over the 16-quarter margin is about \$\TrHybDollar{} million in the predicted cash flow of the next two years. The part attributable to the simulation rather than to combining is a fraction of that: relevant at most for a liquidity plan built on a stated revenue scenario, and negligible for a credit decision.

\paragraph{Examples.} The size of the departures explains the small weight. In 2020, Amazon's revenue over the next eight quarters averaged \ExAmznRev{} above its trailing level; the 16-quarter margin implied cumulative operating cash flow of \ExAmznRule{} of trailing revenue, the twin \ExAmznTwin, and the company reported \ExAmznReal. The twin had the direction right for General Electric in 2016 (rule \ExGeRule, twin \ExGeTwin, realized \ExGeReal) and wrong for Ford and General Motors in 2020. There, revenue changes of \ExFordRev{} and \ExGmRev{} made the twin predict operating cash flow of \ExFordTwin{} and \ExGmTwin{}, while the companies reported \ExFordReal{} and \ExGmReal{} (rules: \ExFordRule, \ExGmRule). Their consolidated cash flows include captive finance operations that a revenue-driven model does not represent. Among firm-years of firms with more than \$2 billion of revenue and departures larger than 8 points, the twin's departure has the sign of the realized one \ExRightSign{} of the time.

\subsection{Where it helps}
Is the twin more useful for highly leveraged firms, firms with high fixed costs or large maturities, or in severe revenue contractions? Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity} gives little support for this. The twin's incremental correlation against the timing regression does not rise with leverage (\HetLevLowTwinReg{} in the lowest tercile, \HetLevHighTwinReg{} in the highest) and differs little across terciles of fixed costs and maturities. Among the \HetDownN{} firm-years with revenue more than 10\% below the trailing level, the twin's incremental correlation against the four-quarter margin (\HetDownTwin) is lower than the 16-quarter margin's (\HetDownSixteen). By cutoff year (Figure~\ref{fig:structural}), the twin's statistic against the four-quarter margin is below the 16-quarter margin's in most years, while its statistic against the timing regression is stable around 0.4.

\subsection{Why the twin overreacts, and a revised specification}
The twin's errors have a direction. In a regression of each eight-quarter total on the revenue change, the twin's operating
cash flow responds \ElastCfoTwin{} percentage points per point of revenue change, against \ElastCfoReal{} in reality, a ratio of
\ElastCfoRatio. EBITDA shows the same excess (\ElastEbitdaRatio{} times); capital expenditure shows none (\ElastCapexRatio). The
overreaction comes from operating leverage: the cost regression is shrunk toward a prior that 35\% of costs are fixed, and firms'
costs are more variable than that.

The sensitivity analysis of the first round had already shown that a 20\% prior lowers the error, so the revised specification was designed with that knowledge. Its rule allowed it only if it was selected without the test year and lowered the
twin's error without lowering its bankruptcy AUC by more than 0.01. We choose the prior each year from a grid
(5\%--50\%, and no prior) using earlier years only. The median error then falls from \RevMaeBase{} to \RevMaeNew{} (difference
\RevDiff, CI \RevDiffLo--\RevDiffHi) and the bankruptcy AUC moves from \RevAucBase{} to \RevAucNew; the revised
specification is \RevAccepted{} and is now the software's default. Selecting the prior within each industry gives the
same error (\RevIndMae). The selected prior is the smallest in the grid in every year, and the unshrunk estimate is close
(\RevNone{} against \RevFive), so the right amount of shrinkage is closer to none than to the original prior. Even revised,
the twin remains less accurate in levels than the 16-quarter margin: the correction removes the overreaction, not the
advantage of persistence.

\section{Results: liquidity decisions and feedback loops}\label{sec:decisions}
Ranking correlations are not what a risk manager uses. Table~\ref{tab:decisions} asks whether, given the revenue that followed, the twin predicts the decisions and events that a liquidity plan addresses. Its event probabilities are weak predictors in absolute terms (AUC 0.56--0.68), comparable with the static runway rule and the opening-balance-sheet score. Out of sample, they add a little to a logit of those rivals: \DecCashDelta{} in AUC for cash falling below half, \DecDebtDelta{} for new borrowing, \DecDivDelta{} for dividend cuts and \DecCapexDelta{} for capital-expenditure cuts, all with confidence intervals above zero.

Table~\ref{tab:loops} evaluates each loop on these outcomes. The result is mechanical but informative about the model's structure. Without the market-access cliff (R2), the twin's cash practically never falls below half its opening level, because the treasury always refinances up to its target; the cash-depletion AUC falls from \DecCashTwin{} to \DecCashNoRtwo. Without cash conservation (B1), dividends are never cut and capital expenditure is cut less often (AUC \DecCapexNoBone{} against \DecCapexTwin). The spread spiral (R1) and delayed restructuring (B2) change no outcome. Two of the four loops are therefore necessary for the model to produce the liquidity events at all; the other two are inert over an eight-quarter horizon. None of them changes the ranking of firms (Section~\ref{sec:ranking}).

The loops also matter for timing. Ranking firms by the twin's probability of a cash depletion within eight quarters
gives a concordance index for the time to the event of \TimCashTwin, against \TimCashNoLoops{} without loops (difference
\TimCashDiff, CI \TimCashDiffLo--\TimCashDiffHi) and \TimCashStatic{} for the static runway; for new borrowing the loops
change nothing (\TimDebtTwin{} against \TimDebtNoLoops). The results hold at other thresholds for each event (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}). By the pre-specified rule --- a gain from the loops in at least two of the four events and in
the timing of at least one --- H3 is \HThreeVerdict.

\section{Forecasts and calibration}\label{sec:forecasts}
For S\&P~500 members (today's and those of 2020), the twin's four-quarter predictive distributions of revenue improve on a seasonal random walk by \FcRevRw{} and on exponential smoothing by \FcRevEts{} in scaled CRPS ($t$ = \FcRevRwT{} and \FcRevEtsT; Table~\ref{tab:fc}). For cash, both benchmarks are better (\FcCashRw{} and \FcCashEts). Blending the twin into the random walk for cash, with the weight chosen on earlier origins, \CashHybVerdict{} the pre-specified rule only trivially. The skill is \CashHybSkill{} (CI \CashHybLo--\CashHybHi) because the selected weight falls from \CashLamHi{} to \CashLamLo{} after 2018: the best cash forecast is the random walk.

Raw 90\% intervals are too narrow: they cover \CalRevRaw{} of revenue outcomes and \CalCashRaw{} of cash outcomes. Online conformal rescaling restores coverage to \CalRevConf{} and \CalCashConf{} on average (Table~\ref{tab:cal}, Figure~\ref{fig:cal}). It uses only errors whose target quarter ended at least 75 days before the forecast origin; by then most quarters, including fourth quarters reported in 10-Ks, have been filed. The question is whether this holds where it matters. It holds across horizons and firm sizes, but not in the stress year: for quarters from 2020Q2 to 2021Q1, conformal revenue intervals cover only \CalRevStress. The recalibration learns the average error of quiet years and cannot anticipate a regime break. For cash, the raw intervals do not widen for smaller firms ($t$ = \CalCashWidthSizeT{} for size in a regression of interval width) although their errors are larger ($t$ = \CalCashErrSizeT): the uncertainty model is too homogeneous across firms.

We tested two remedies against a pre-specified target of 85--95\% coverage in every group (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:cal2}). Rescaling by size and volatility tercile, with factors learned from reported errors, gives
\CalCondCash{} coverage for cash and \CalCondRev{} for revenue; it \CalCondCashOk{} the target for cash and
\CalCondRevOk{} it for revenue. Adaptive conformal inference \citep{gibbs2021} reaches \CalAciCash{} and \CalAciRev{} and
\CalAciRevOk{} the target for revenue. Neither fixes the regime break: for the 2020Q2--2021Q1 quarters revenue coverage
is \CalCondRevStress{} with group scaling and \CalAciRevStress{} with adaptive conformal inference, which can only react after the
errors are reported.

\section{Sensitivity and hindsight}\label{sec:sensitivity}
Several parameters are judgment calls rather than estimates: the credit line assumed when none is tagged, the prior on the fixed-cost share and its weight, the shrinkage of trend growth and cyclicality, the coverage thresholds of the market-access cliff, the speed of restructuring, and the rating-spread table. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The hypotheses.} H1 is rejected: the twin's stress and baseline scores do not rank distress better than the trailing FCF margin or than its own scoring formula without simulation. H2 is accepted by the pre-specified rule, with a small economic magnitude. H3 is \HThreeVerdict{} by its pre-specified rule: two of the four loops are necessary for the model to produce the liquidity events and slightly improve their prediction and timing; the other two have no measurable effect.

\paragraph{Statistical, predictive and decision value.} The three are different, and the evidence separates them. The twin's transmission mechanics are statistically informative beyond persistence (a $t$-statistic near 10 in the encompassing regressions). Their predictive value is small. Added with a weight of \TrLambdaLo--\TrLambdaHi{} to the best simple rule, they lower its error by a few percent; except for FCF, a simple rule added in the same way delivers most of that gain. Their decision value lies where a simple rule has no answer at all: the timing of a breach when maturities and credit lines interact, the amount of new borrowing a stated scenario requires, whether a dividend survives. On those questions the twin is a structured calculator whose inputs are estimated and audited from filings, and its advantage over a static runway calculation is small but positive.

\paragraph{What the twin is for.} A filing-based twin is not an early-warning system: its ranking of firms is that of its own scoring formula. It is a tool for conditional analysis of a specified shock, and its output should be shrunk toward persistence. In practice, a cash forecast should start from the long-window margin and adjust a fraction of the way toward the simulation.

\paragraph{External validity.} The universe is US non-financial SEC filers with at least \$50 million of revenue. The pipeline depends on standardized, tagged, point-in-time disclosure, which exists in a comparable form only under SEC XBRL and, since 2020--21, the European Single Electronic Format for annual reports of listed companies (without quarterly data). Among the \SmallN{} firm-years of firms with \$10--50 million of revenue, the pattern is the same: the twin's bankruptcy AUC is \SmallAucTwin{} against \SmallAucFormula{} for its formula (\SmallBk{} petitions), and its median cash-flow error is \SmallMaeTwin{} against \SmallMaeSixteen{} for the 16-quarter margin. Private firms report less often and less consistently, which affects calibration more than structure: the stock-and-flow core and the accounting identity carry over, but parameters estimated from 16 quarters would have to come from fewer annual observations, with more weight on priors. Banks and insurers need a different balance-sheet model. Market-based inputs, which structural credit models use, are a natural extension for listed firms.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The transmission test uses realized revenue, so it cannot separate errors in the model's mechanics from noise in reported cash flow. Bankruptcy is observed only through 8-K filings; firms that stop filing before petitioning, or that are acquired in distress, are not counted. Outcomes for firms that disappear are missing, so the transmission and burn tests condition on survival for eight quarters. Some XBRL items are tagged idiosyncratically.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We built a transparent, automatically calibrated simulation model of the firm and asked what it knows that simple rules do not. Most of what it appeared to know was persistence, and the test that first suggested otherwise measured mean reversion. What remains is small and specific: given a revenue path, the direction of the simulation's departures from a long-window cash-flow margin is informative in an encompassing regression, and adding a small fraction of them to the margin improves FCF predictions out of sample by more than any simple rule does. The simulation does not help to rank firms by distress. These answers come from a design to which any simulation model of the firm can be held: a ladder of simple rivals, an encompassing test and an out-of-sample combination, on a survivorship-free panel with a census of bankruptcy petitions. The data, code and tests are public so that other models can be held to it.

\section{Implementation}
The Python reference engine (NumPy) performs calibration, experiments and exports. A line-for-line TypeScript port runs in a Web Worker, so the public dashboard needs no server. Golden runs exported from Python with identical pre-drawn shocks must agree to $10^{-6}$; the balance-sheet identity is checked by property-based tests; the data pipeline, including 52/53-week fiscal calendars, is tested end to end on synthetic XBRL; and a synthetic test checks that the transmission statistics reject a predictor that is merely smoother than the base rule. The research sample (\UniverseN{} companies) is run in 16 shards on free GitHub-hosted runners.
